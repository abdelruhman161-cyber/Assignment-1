Which Pages Will Lose Search Impressions Next Month? A Client-Grouped, Leakage-Checked Refresh-Priority Model

Abdelruhman Omran · FlyRank ML Internship capstone · October 2026
DataMethodologyResults LimitationsRecommendationsReproducibility
Abstract.

Content teams cannot review every page, so they need a defensible order in which to look at them. We ask which pages are most likely to lose more than 20% of their daily search impressions in the following month, using only information available at the end of the previous month. We build features from March 2026 search data, label each page from April 2026, and compare a simple momentum baseline, a logistic regression and a gradient-boosting model, always holding out whole clients. Across five client-grouped folds the gradient-boosting model reaches a ROC AUC of 0.72 ± 0.03, against 0.70 ± 0.02 for the baseline, a modest but mostly consistent gain. On one held-out split of 11 clients, 90% of the pages in the highest-risk decile did lose more than 20% of their impressions, against 50% overall. We present the result as a review queue with reason codes, observed and directional, not as proof of what causes a decline.

1. Introduction and problem statement

A search-content team has a limited number of hours each month. The decision this work supports is simple: which pages should be reviewed for a refresh first? A page that is already healthy does not need attention; a page that is about to lose visibility does. Ranking pages by risk turns a long list into a short queue.

The question is framed as a prediction, so the answer can be checked: standing at the end of March, which pages will see their daily impressions fall by more than 20% in April? Because the answer is known one month later, the model can be tested honestly instead of argued about.

2. Data

The analysis uses the pseudonymized FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release (build flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703). All client, page and keyword identifiers are hashed; no client names, domains, URLs or queries appear in this paper or in its outputs.

Why restrict to clients present in April? If a client simply has no April data, a page would look like it lost 100% of its impressions. That would be missing data, not a real decline.

3. Methodology

Label

For each page, the average daily impressions in April (30 days) are divided by the average daily impressions in March (31 days). The page is labeled 1 if that ratio is below 0.80, that is, a fall of more than 20%. A page with no April rows, in a client that does have April data, counts as zero. The label is positive for 49.7% of pages.

Features

Every feature is computed from March only, so every one of them exists at the decision moment (31 March).

FeatureMeaning
Impressions, clicks, CTRMarch totals (logged) and clicks ÷ impressions
Average positionImpression-weighted average Search position in March
Impressions and clicks momentumSecond half of March (days 16–31) against the first half, per day, on a log scale
Share of client impressionsThe page’s share of its client’s March impressions (logged)
Active daysNumber of March days with data
Content ageDays between the page’s creation date and 31 March

Baseline and models

The baseline is deliberately simple and strong: rank pages by how fast their impressions were already falling inside March. Against it we fit a logistic regression and a histogram gradient-boosting classifier (200 iterations, learning rate 0.08, depth 4, fixed seed).

Validation design and leakage checks

  • Grouped by client. No client appears on both sides of any split, so the model is tested on clients it has never seen. A random row split would let one client’s habits leak across the boundary. The primary split puts 93,085 pages from 33 clients in training and 8,356 pages from 11 clients in testing.
  • Five-fold cross-validation by client, because a single split with 11 test clients can be lucky or unlucky.
  • Time-aware by construction. Features end on 31 March; the label begins on 1 April.
  • A deliberate leakage trap. We add one column built from April impressions, watch the score jump, then delete it (Section 4).

4. Results

On the single split the boosting model scores 0.776, the baseline 0.673 and the logistic regression 0.655. The cross-validated figures are lower and are the ones we trust: 0.723 ± 0.030 for boosting and 0.697 ± 0.017 for the baseline. Boosting beats the baseline in four of the five folds and loses narrowly in the fifth. Per-fold boosting AUC: 0.700, 0.719, 0.770, 0.729, 0.696. The improvement is real but modest; the simple momentum rule already carries much of the signal.

The ranking behaves the way a review queue should. In the top decile, 90% of pages did decline, against 50% overall and 15% in the lowest-risk decile. Precision in the top 10% was 90% for the model and 64% for the baseline on this split (lift 1.9× and 1.4×). These queue figures come from the single split of 11 clients and should be read as directional.

The leakage trap

With the label-derived column the AUC reached 1.000; without it, 0.776. The column was deleted and nothing after this point uses it. A score that looks too good is a reason to audit the features, not to celebrate.

What drives the score

Recent impressions momentum matters most, followed by content age. Position, active days and clicks momentum add little. These are associations in this data, not causes.

5. Limitations and honest framing

  • Observed and directional. The findings describe what happened in these clients’ data between March and April 2026. They do not prove why a page lost impressions, and they say nothing about how Google’s algorithm works.
  • No causal claim about refreshing. Nothing here shows that refreshing a flagged page will recover its impressions. The queue says where to look first, not what will work.
  • One month pair. March to April can contain seasonality. Some “declines” may be a calendar effect rather than page decay.
  • A noisy label. About half of all pages cross the 20% threshold. Pages with few impressions move a lot by chance, and a high March can fall back simply by regression to the mean.
  • A small, uneven test. The primary split has 11 test clients, and in one cross-validation fold a single large client made up the whole test set. That is why the headline number is the cross-validated one, and why the fold-to-fold spread (± 0.03) matters.
  • Top-decile figures come from one split and were not repeated across folds.
  • Coverage. Only 44 clients have March pages with 100+ impressions; findings may not transfer to clients with little data or a later tracking start.
  • Sealed month untouched. A final check on the sealed test month (2026-06) remains a separate step.

6. Ranked recommendations — the action playbook

Use the model as a review queue, not an automatic rewrite order. A person should look at each flagged page before anything changes.

Start with deciles 1 and 2, which fell in about 90% and 81% of cases. Deciles 3–5 are worth monitoring. Deciles 6–10 mostly held; protect them and avoid unnecessary edits.

Step 2 — Use the reason codes to decide what to look at

Each page in the highest-risk decile (836 pages on the held-out split) carries one or more reason codes. A page can have several.

Step 3 — Track what happens

Record which flagged pages were reviewed and what changed, then compare with a matched set of unflagged pages the following month. That is the only way to learn whether a refresh helped; this study did not test it.
